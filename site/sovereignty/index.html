Sovereign data, on your terms.

Why Europe’s data platforms belong inside Europe’s own perimeter, and how open source makes leaving anyone possible.

Opening: this talk is about control. Not about cloud versus on-premises, but about who decides what happens to your data, under which law, and whether you can leave.

Europe, independent

Europe can build its own.

450 million people. 27 countries. Our data should answer to our laws, our values and our choices.

The emotional core of the talk. Europe is not too small or too late: it is the largest single market in the world's democracies. The question is not whether we can, but whether we decide to.

Location is not jurisdiction.

2018

US CLOUD Act

US authorities can compel US providers to hand over data they control, wherever it is stored, including in an EU region.

renewed 2024

FISA Section 702

Allows surveillance of non-US persons’ communications held by US providers.

2020 → 2023

Schrems II, then DPF

The EU’s top court struck down Privacy Shield. Its successor, the 2023 Data Privacy Framework, is challenged again.

An EU region of a US provider keeps the bytes in Europe. It does not put them under European law alone.

Facts only: the CLOUD Act was enacted in March 2018. FISA Section 702 was reauthorised by Congress in April 2024. The CJEU invalidated Privacy Shield in July 2020 (Schrems II); the EU-US Data Privacy Framework adequacy decision followed in July 2023 and is being challenged. The point is legal exposure, not bad intent.

2025, in four months

Make your data Trump-proof.

January

Oversight removed

The White House dismissed the Democratic members of the PCLOB, the US privacy board the EU–US data deal relies on.

February

Sanctions by order

An executive order sanctioned the International Criminal Court. Its chief prosecutor was later reported to have lost his US-hosted email.

April

Tariffs overnight

Sweeping tariffs on EU goods were announced in a single afternoon.

It is not about one president. It is about who can switch you off.

Facts, all checkable: PCLOB members dismissed 27 January 2025; Executive Order 14203 sanctioning the ICC, 6 February 2025; AP reported in May 2025 that prosecutor Karim Khan lost access to his email; the 2 April 2025 tariff announcement set 20 % on EU goods, then paused for 90 days. Keep it calm: any government can change rules by order. The lesson is dependency, not party politics.

Lock-in is built in four layers.

The control plane

The vendor runs the brain of your platform. If they stop, you stop.

The format

Proprietary tables and metadata only one engine can read well.

The licence

“Open” projects relicensed to source-available after adoption.

The exit cost

Egress fees and rewrites make leaving a multi-year project.

Each layer alone is manageable. Together they decide your roadmap for you. The EU Data Act, applicable since September 2025, pushes against the last one: cloud switching charges must end by January 2027. It does nothing about the other three.

Sovereignty is four tests you can check.

Location

Data, catalog and engines run in your perimeter.

Control

You operate it. No vendor control plane, no phone-home.

Portability

Open formats. You can leave on any day.

Continuity

Permissive licences. If anyone walks away, you fork.

Ask every platform, including ours, to pass all four.

This is the frame for the rest of the talk. Each test is a yes or no question a buyer can verify without trusting marketing.

Open source is the exit door. Only if it opens.

stardelt and its stack: Apache 2.0, MIT or BSD, with one documented, optional MPL 2.0 exception.

Licences decide whether the fourth test, continuity, is real. A fork of a permissively licensed project is a working exit; a fork of a source-available one is a legal question.

Real portability

Uninstall stardelt. Your tables still open.

Every table is Apache Iceberg and Parquet on your own S3 storage. Any Iceberg engine reads it tomorrow, with no export and no conversion.

This is the slide to remember. Portability is not an export button; it is a format nobody owns.

One interface. Open engines. Your infrastructure.

stardelt composes proven open engines around one open table format, and adds what they lack together: one interface, one API, one sign-in and permissions by team. Logos are trademarks of their owners.

Europe has done this before.

1954

CERN

Twelve countries pooled their science. In 1989 the web was invented there.

1970

Airbus

Built against a giant. Today the world’s largest maker of airliners.

1991

Linux

A student project in Helsinki. Today it runs most of the world’s servers.

2016

Galileo

Europe’s own satellite navigation, free for everyone to use.

Data platforms are next.

Every one of these looked impossible at the start and became infrastructure the whole world uses. Open source data platforms are the same kind of project: shared, public, owned by nobody and usable by everybody.

Independence is not a wall.

It is the freedom to choose, and to leave.

Sovereignty is often heard as isolation. It is the opposite: open formats and open licences let you work with anyone, because you are never forced to stay.
